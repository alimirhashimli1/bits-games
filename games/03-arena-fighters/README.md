# 🥊 Game 03: Arena Fighters

A one-on-one versus fighting game inspired by **Street Fighter II** (1991). Pick one of fifteen fighters and battle the CPU, a friend on the same keyboard, or anyone online by sending them a link. All characters, story and art are original.

## Story

Every ten years the **Iron Crown** tournament is held in the tower city of **Vanmoor**, and every ten years its host, **Magnus Vane**, keeps the crown. This time fifteen fighters answer the call, each with their own reason to take it from him. Only one can fight through the rest to reach the top of his tower.

## Fighters

Fifteen playable fighters, each with two special moves and a home arena, plus the boss. Every fighter's specials are entered the same way, with a punch (P = light or heavy punch; heavy gives the stronger version):

- **Special 1: → + P.** On the keyboard, player 1 taps D, then F or G.
- **Special 2: ↓ → + P.** Player 1 taps S, D, then F or G, one straight after the other. Nothing needs holding.

Directions are written for player 1, who starts on the left. They stay the same when the fighters swap sides: player 1's → is always D, and player 2, who starts on the right, enters them the other way round (their → is always ←).

## How it plays

- **Best of three rounds**, 99 seconds each, unless the options say otherwise. The fighter with more health when time runs out wins the round. A double KO or equal health is a draw round.
- **Four attack buttons:** light punch, heavy punch, light kick, heavy kick. Standing, crouching and jumping versions of each.
- **Blocking:** hold away from the opponent. Standing blocks stop high attacks and overheads, crouching blocks stop low attacks. Blocked special moves still chip a little health. Crouching also ducks under projectiles thrown flat at chest height (fireballs, cards, syringes, flames, bullets); ones that hit low, like Kanan's shockwave and Rajab's roulette wheel, and Osal's lobbed grenade still catch a crouching fighter.
- **Throws:** light punch + light kick up close. Pressing the same buttons at the same moment escapes the throw.
- **Special moves** use the same two short inputs for every fighter (see the table above): → + punch, and ↓ → + punch. They do not need frame-perfect timing, and they never swap round when the fighters cross over.
- **Combos:** some normal attacks can be cancelled into special moves on hit or block, and a combo counter shows the hits.
- **Knockdowns:** heavy hits, throws and some specials knock the opponent down. They get up after a fixed time and cannot be hit while they are down.
- **Game modes:**
  - **Arcade:** fight seven of the other fighters in a shuffled order, then Magnus Vane, with a story intro and an ending for each fighter. Continue after a loss.
  - **VS CPU:** a single match against the computer at a difficulty you choose. Pick your fighter, then pick the one the computer plays — or take the `?` box and have it choose for you. The arena is drawn at random, so the same pairing is not always fought on the same ground. Magnus Vane can be neither picked nor drawn; the rest of the roster and every arena can.
  - **Versus:** two players on one keyboard, one keyboard plus a gamepad, or two gamepads.
  - **Online:** one player picks their fighter, gets a room code and a link, and sends the link however they like. The other opens it, picks their fighter, and the match starts. Both sides have to agree to a rematch. There are no accounts, and nothing is stored on a server: the two browsers talk to each other directly, and the signalling server only introduces them.
- **Sixteen arenas**, one for each fighter plus the tower roof. Each has animated crowds and background details. In versus mode any of them can be picked, by whoever lost the last match.
- **Options** (from the title): how well the computer plays, how long a round lasts (30, 60 or 99 seconds), how many rounds make a match (best of one, three or five) and whether there is any sound. They are remembered in this browser, so the game opens the way it was left. In an online match the rules cannot be two things at once, so the room is played by its host, whose options settle it for both.

## Controls

The keyboard is shared in versus mode, so each player gets their own half. Gamepads work for either player: in versus the first pad to connect is player 1 and the second is player 2. Keyboards differ in how many keys they register at once, so two players on one keyboard may find some combinations do not come through; a gamepad avoids that.

| Action | Player 1 | Player 2 | Gamepad |
|--------|----------|----------|---------|
| Move / jump / crouch | W A S D | ← ↑ → ↓ | D-pad / left stick |
| Light / heavy punch | F / G | K / L | X / Y |
| Light / heavy kick | V / B | , / . | A / B |
| Pause | Esc | Esc | Start |
| Mute sound and music | M | M | — |
| Back to console menu | Backspace or the `< MENU` button | | — |

In arcade, VS CPU and online mode, the single player uses the Player 1 keys or any gamepad.

## Technical plan

- **Engine:** Phaser (already installed) draws the game and runs the menus. The fight itself does **not** use Phaser physics.
- **Scene flow** (`src/scenes/`): Boot → Title → Mode Select → Character Select → (Online lobby) → (Story) → VS → Fight → Results → (Continue), which offers a rematch, new fighters or the title. The title also opens two screens of its own, the options and the controls table, and a fight can be paused onto a menu laid over it — in every mode but online. Esc steps back one screen at a time. The character select picks both sides in versus; in arcade and online it picks only this player's fighter, since arcade draws its opponents from the ladder and online gets them from the other browser. Arcade adds two screens of its own: the fighter's story before the first rung and after the boss, and the continue count after a lost fight. Online adds the lobby, where the room is opened or joined and the two browsers agree on the match; a page opened from a room link starts on the fighter select rather than the title, since the mode is already settled. A `MatchSetup` (`src/systems/matchSetup.ts`: the mode, both fighters, the arena and the match rules) is handed from scene to scene, so a rematch or "change fighters" starts from the last choices. Screens without a menu read their keys through `ScreenInput`, which ignores its first frame like the shared menu does, so a gamepad button still held from the last screen cannot skip the next one. The character select needs a cursor per player rather than one shared one, so it reads `SELECT_CONTROLS` directly and ignores its own first frame the same way.
- **Resolution:** 320×180 pixels through `@shared/phaser/createPixelGame`, the same as games 01 and 02.
- **Fight simulation** (`src/systems/sim/`): the fight is a pure, fixed-step simulation that runs at 60 steps per second. Each step takes the current state and one input per player and returns the next state. The simulation does not read the clock, `Math.random` or anything else from Phaser.
  - **Whole numbers only.** Positions and speeds are integers in sub-pixels (1 pixel = 256 units), and only `+`, `-`, `*` and truncated division are used on them. Two browsers given the same inputs therefore compute the same fight, frame for frame, which is what makes online play possible.
  - **No randomness inside.** Anything random, such as the CPU's choices, happens outside the simulation and reaches it as inputs, so there is no random seed to keep in step between two browsers.
  - **One step** (`stepFight.ts`): both fighters move from their own input (`movement.ts`: walking, slower backwards, crouching, and jumps whose direction is fixed at take-off), then the bodies are sorted out (`pushboxes.ts`): overlapping push boxes are shared out half each, fighters who end up more than a screen apart are pulled back by whoever moved away, the arena walls hold them in, and a fighter pinned against a wall makes the other take the whole push. A fighter in a wall leap who has reached the edge behind them then springs off it (`wallLeaps.ts`). Last, fighters on the ground turn to face each other, unless they are in the middle of a move, so a dash that carries a fighter past the opponent keeps going the same way. In the air the push box starts above the feet, which is what lets a jump clear an opponent.
  - **The clock** (`fixedStepClock.ts`) turns each screen frame into a whole number of steps, so the fight runs at the same speed on any refresh rate. The camera (`camera.ts`) is worked out from the state and centred between the fighters, and it stops at the arena's ends.
  - **Replay check:** while the fight was being built, the scene recorded every step's inputs and a dev key replayed them from the first state, comparing a checksum (`checksum.ts`, FNV-1a over the state's numbers) with the live fight: the two had to agree exactly, or the simulation was not the pure function online play needs it to be. The tool came out with the rest of the development overlay once the game was finished; the checksum stayed, and is what catches a desync in online play.
  - Phaser scenes only **draw** the current state: sprites, the HUD, camera and effects.
- **Inputs** (`src/systems/input/`): every source, whether keyboard half, gamepad, CPU or online opponent, is turned into one small **bitmask per frame** (4 directions and 4 buttons). The simulation cannot tell who is playing, so the CPU cannot cheat and an online opponent is handled like any other player.
  - The bits are four directions and the four buttons (`inputBits.ts`). Left + right held together cancel out and up + down counts as up, so nobody can walk forward and block at once. A button tapped and let go between two frames still counts as pressed.
  - `LocalInput` reads one player's half of the keyboard and their gamepad (`PLAYER_CONTROLS` in `config.ts`). The shared `ActionInput` gained an optional **gamepad slot** for this: without one, every pad counts, which is what menus want; with one, a player listens only to their own pad. With one player at the keyboard (arcade, online), any pad drives player 1.
  - The **input history** (the last 64 steps per player) lives in the fight state, so both players online read motions from exactly the same data.
  - **Motions** (`motions.ts`) are read in keypad numbers: 5 is neutral, 6 forward, 2 down. There are only two, shared by every fighter so their specials are all entered the same way: **6** (→) and **26** (↓ →). Each player's motions are read from the side they start the round on (`MOTION_FACING`), not the way their fighter faces, so they never swap round when the fighters cross over. A motion's directions must come one straight after the other, with at most 5 steps between them (a diagonal on the way counts towards that), all within half a second, and its last direction must have been pressed within 20 steps of the button, not held. So walking forward into a punch is a normal, and so is crouching, pausing and then tapping → + punch, while S, D and F rolled one into the next is ↓ → + P.
  - ↓ → also finishes →, so when both are finished the special on the longer motion comes out (`motionLength`), and the shorter one only when the longer one cannot be used now, such as a second projectile.
  - **Input debug view:** **I** in a fight shows each player's held direction and buttons, and their last press with the motions read with it.
- **Frame data** (`src/content/fighters/`): every move is data: startup, active and recovery frames, damage, hitstun, blockstun, pushback, how it must be blocked (high, low or overhead) and what it can cancel into. Every number lives there or in `config.ts`.
  - **A move is a list of segments** (`moves.ts`): a pose held for a number of steps. Segments that can hit name a **strike**: the limb it lands with (a fist, a foot, or the head for a headbutt) and the size of the box around it. A command throw's segments are marked as a **grab** instead. Startup is everything before the first strike or grab, active is the striking and grabbing segments, recovery is what follows. A fighter's moves are typed against the list of twelve normals, so none can be left out. Brand's are in `brand/brandMoves.ts`: a jab is 3 steps of startup, 3 active and 6 of recovery, while his roundhouse takes 8 to come out and 16 to recover.
  - **Hurtboxes come from the pose** (`src/systems/sim/boxes.ts`): the head, the torso and each limb of whatever pose a fighter is in become a box, worked out once per pose from its joint positions and the body's thickness. An outstretched leg can be hit, which is what makes a missed heavy kick punishable, and every fighter gets boxes that fit their build with nothing drawn by hand. The **hitbox** sits on the striking fist or foot of the active pose. All of it is whole numbers, so both players online get the same boxes.
  - **Starting a move** (`src/systems/sim/attacks.ts`): a newly pressed button starts the standing move, the crouching one if ↓ is held, or the jumping one in the air, once per jump. When buttons are pressed together the heaviest wins. A fighter stays put for the length of a ground move, and landing ends a jumping one. The pose a fighter is in (`pose.ts`) is part of the fight, since it decides their hurtboxes; the sprite simply shows it.
  - `src/content/fighters/fighterData.ts` holds each built fighter's art, moves, specials, CPU style and **movement**: walking speeds forwards and back, jump height and jump drift (`fighterMovement.ts`). Brand uses the standard numbers from `config.ts`; others adjust them. Gravity is the same for everyone. Fighters not built yet stand in as Brand, in looks and in moves.
- **Arcade mode** (`src/systems/arcade.ts`, stories in `src/content/story/`): a run up a ladder of eight.
  - **The ladder** is seven of the other fourteen fighters in a shuffled order, drawn without replacement, and then Magnus Vane. So nobody is met twice, nobody fights themselves, and the boss is always last. An `ArcadeRun` (the player's fighter, the ladder, the rung and the continues spent) rides along inside the `MatchSetup`, so every scene already has it and nothing new is threaded through.
  - **Rising difficulty:** a CPU level per rung — easy, easy, normal, normal, normal, hard, hard — and then the boss, who forces his own level in the fight whatever the rung says, so the ladder needs no special case for him.
  - **Each rung is fought in the opponent's home arena**, the way the tournament goes to meet each fighter where they live, ending on the tower roof.
  - **What follows a fight is worked out away from the drawing** (`arcadeOutcome`): winning moves up a rung, winning the last one plays the ending, and anything else — a loss or a draw game — goes to the continue. The results screen only acts on that answer, which is why it can be tested without a browser.
  - **Continues** (`ContinueScene`): a count runs down from nine while the player decides. Taking one fights the same opponent again with a continue against the run's name; giving up, or letting the count reach zero, ends the run at the title. The ladder is never reshuffled, so the fighter who beat them is still the one waiting.
  - **Stories** (`arcadeStory.ts`): every fighter has a reason for entering, shown before the first rung, and an ending, shown once Vane falls, each at most 4 lines of 38 characters — what fits the screen in the pixel font. They are typed `Record<PlayableId, …>`, so a fighter added without a story will not compile and nobody can reach the top of the tower and be met with an empty screen. The lines type themselves out with the shared `Typewriter`, left-aligned on one edge since centring each line would make it creep sideways as it fills; confirm shows them all at once, and confirm again moves on. After the ending the game returns to the title, the way an arcade cabinet resets.
  - **The character select no longer picks the CPU's fighter** in arcade: the ladder does. That made arcade the same shape as online — one cursor, this player's fighter only — and the two-stage cursor it used before came out.
- **VS CPU** (`src/systems/soloMatch.ts`): one match against the computer, with no ladder, no story and no continues behind it.
  - **The player chooses both fighters; the arena is drawn.** The mode select asks one extra question — easy, normal or hard — then the fighter select is worked twice with one cursor, the player's own fighter and then the computer's, and `soloMatch` draws only the ground they meet on. Leaving the arena to the draw is what keeps a second match against the same opponent from being the same fight. The draw is a pure function of a seed, like the arcade shuffle, so it can be run thousands of times in a test without a browser: every arena came up, and a match with an opponent chosen always fought that opponent.
  - **`?` is a box on the grid, not a button beside it** (`randomPick.ts`): it sits in the sixteenth cell, drawn like a portrait and reached with the same cursor movement, so there is nothing new to learn. Confirming it draws a fighter and **moves the cursor onto them**, so the player is shown who they were given instead of finding out on the versus screen. It draws from `FIGHTER_IDS`, the fifteen anyone may pick, so it can never hand out the boss. Twenty thousand draws gave all fifteen, evenly (1267–1411 each), and Vane none. The results screen keeps a **RANDOM OPPONENT** entry for the same thing in one press.
  - **Magnus Vane is left out of the draw** but every arena is left in, so the tower roof is the one part of his stage that can be seen without climbing the ladder. Meeting Vane himself at a difficulty the player picked would both surprise them and stop him being earned.
  - **A mirror match is a fair draw.** Player 2 already wears the fighter's alternate colours when both sides are the same, so nothing special is needed; it comes up about one time in fifteen.
  - **The results screen offers a fresh draw** (RANDOM OPPONENT) as well as the same fight again, and it goes through the same `soloMatchFor` the fighter select does, so picking an opponent and asking for a drawn one cannot drift apart.
  - **Modes are asked about, not compared by name** (`hasCpuOpponent`, `isTwoPlayer` in `matchSetup.ts`). A fourth mode would otherwise have to be added to seven scattered comparisons against one mode's name, and any one missed would fail silently — a solo match with no CPU driving player 2 would simply stand there. Now each question is answered in one place.
- **Choosing the arena** (`ArenaSelectScene`, `src/scenes/select/ArenaGrid.ts`): after the fighters comes the ground they meet on, in every mode where somebody is there to choose it. Versus asks the two players in turn (below), VS CPU asks the one player once the opponent is settled, and online asks the player hosting the room. Arcade is the exception: the ladder brings each rung to its own opponent's home arena, which is part of what makes a run feel like a tour.
  - **The loser picks the arena.** Player 1 chooses the first match, since nobody has lost yet, and after that the choice goes to whoever just lost; a draw game hands it over rather than leaving it where it was. Nobody has to agree with anybody, the player who is behind gets the say, and a player who keeps winning stops choosing the ground they keep winning on. The rule itself is `nextArenaPicker` in `matchSetup.ts`, away from the screen that uses it, so it can be checked without a browser — it was, including the case where one player wins six in a row and never gets the pick back.
  - **The thumbnails are painted from the arenas** (`arenaThumbnails.ts`), not drawn a second time by hand. Each is the arena sampled at 48 × 27 — the screen's own shape — front layer first, falling back through to the sky, so a stage can never be picked from a picture that has stopped matching it. The crowd is left out: at that size people are a pixel each and only muddy the colours that make a stage recognisable. They come to 17 to 30 colours each and all sixteen came out different from one another.
  - **The cursor is the choosing player's colour** in versus, and their half of the controls drives it, so on a rematch it is clear at a glance whose turn it is without reading the heading. Where one player is choosing — VS CPU, or the host of an online room — the screen says SELECT ARENA instead of naming a player, either gamepad drives the cursor, and it opens on the arena the match already had: the one drawn for a VS CPU match, so the draw becomes a suggestion rather than a decision.
  - **Rematch goes back through the arena select** in versus, which is what makes the loser's pick mean anything; CHANGE FIGHTERS goes back a screen further. A VS CPU rematch keeps the arena its player chose, and so does RANDOM OPPONENT, which was asked for another opponent and not for another place to fight. Online keeps the arena it was given, since only one side may ever decide that and it is not settled here.
- **The boss is kept out of the roster** (`src/content/roster.ts`): `FIGHTERS` is the fifteen anyone can pick, in character-select order, and Magnus Vane sits beside it as `BOSS`. `PlayableId` is one of the fifteen and `FighterId` is either, so the character select, the fighter scroll and the arena choice are typed against the fifteen and cannot offer him, while a `MatchSetup` can still name him for the last fight of arcade mode. The checksum numbers fighters by `ALL_FIGHTER_IDS` (the fifteen and then the boss), so he has a number of his own rather than sharing "not found" with everyone unknown.
- **Fifteen fighters without fifteen copies:** a fighter is a data folder (`src/content/fighters/<name>/`) with a body, poses, frame data, specials and CPU tendencies. Walking, jumping, blocking, hit reactions, throws and knockdowns are shared by everyone, so a new fighter is only the things that make them different. Special moves are built from a small set of shared behaviours (projectile, rising attack, dash attack, command throw, dive, teleport, counter), each tuned by numbers.
- **Combat rules** (`src/systems/sim/`, numbers in `COMBAT` in `config.ts` and in each strike):
  - **Status** (`fightState.ts`): a fighter is free, reeling (hitstun), holding a block (blockstun), knocked down (falling, lying, rising), throwing or being thrown. Only a free fighter answers their controls; the rest count down (`status.ts`). Health, a pushback slide and the combo count are part of the state too, and all of it goes into the checksum.
  - **Strikes** carry their damage, hitstun, blockstun, pushback and **guard**: a mid is blocked standing or crouching, a low only crouching, an overhead only standing. Every jumping attack is an overhead.
  - **Landing a blow** (`hits.ts`): both fighters' strikes are checked on the same step, so two blows landing together both count. A move hits once. **Blocking** is holding away from the attacker on the ground while free and not attacking, or while already blocking; there is no blocking in the air. A hit cancels the defender's own move; a hit in the air, a knockdown strike or the last of their health knocks them down. At 0 health the fighter is knocked out and stays down.
  - **Pushback:** the one hit or blocking slides away, slowing each step. Against the wall they cannot go back, so the attacker slides back instead.
  - **Hitstop:** both fighters freeze for 7 steps on a hit and 5 on a block. Inputs are still recorded during the freeze, so a motion can be entered in it, and buttons pressed in it are kept aside (`buffered` in the state) and count as fresh presses when it ends. That is how a special move is cancelled out of a normal that has just hit: the motion goes in during the freeze.
  - **Knockdowns:** off their feet in an arc, then lying for 30 steps and rising for 20. There are no hurtboxes at any point of it, so nothing can hit a fighter who is down.
  - **Throws** (`throws.ts`): light punch and light kick within 3 steps of each other, near a standing opponent who is not reeling or blocking. The first of the two presses has usually started a light normal, so a throw may take over during that move's first steps. The one thrown can break free with the same buttons during the first 10 steps; both then let go, are pushed apart and hold their guard for a moment, so the buttons that broke the throw do not also attack. Two throws at once break each other. A clean throw costs 120 health and knocks down. Nobody can be thrown during the invulnerable start of a rising special or while passing through in a dash. The throw status names which throw is under way (the ordinary one or a command throw), and the checksum includes it.
  - **Cancels** (`attacks.ts`): once a normal has connected and is past its startup, it can be cut short. Light normals (`chain`) cancel into another light normal or a special move, which is how three crouching jabs chain into a combo; Brand's standing and crouching heavy punches (`special`) cancel into special moves only; his heavy kicks do not cancel. Light attacks push back only a little, so a chain stays in reach.
- **Special moves** (`src/content/fighters/specials.ts`, each fighter's in their own folder, such as `brand/brandSpecials.ts`):
  - A special is a motion, its move segments and one shared **behaviour** tuned by numbers. Specials are always started with a punch, so the kicks never give one: the light or heavy punch picks the light or heavy version of those numbers. There are nine behaviours, and with Sable every one of them is now in use: **projectile**, **rising**, **dive**, **wall leap**, **dash**, **command throw**, **counter**, **teleport** and **heal** (the heal is the one no fighter uses). A projectile can start a few pixels above its limb (`upPx`), which is how a shockwave rolls along the floor rather than half inside it, and its strike carries its own guard, so a low one must be blocked crouching.
  - **Starting one:** when a punch is pressed, the special whose motion was just completed comes out, the longer motion winning when both were. Otherwise the button gives a normal. Specials start from the ground, from a free fighter or as a cancel. Walking forward into a punch, a crouch that ends well before the →, or a motion finished too long before the button all give a normal.
  - **Projectiles** (`src/systems/sim/projectiles.ts`) are part of the fight state. One leaves the hand on its move's spawn step, flies at its own speed (the heavy version faster), and is used up when it touches its target, who blocks it or takes it like any other strike. Blocked specials **chip** a little health. A crouching fighter ducks under any projectile that flies level and does not hit low (`passesOver`): it carries on past them. Lobbed projectiles (`arc`) and low ones still hit. Two projectiles from different players that touch cancel each other out, and a fighter can only have one out at a time: the motion then gives a normal instead.
  - **Rising specials** launch the fighter on their launch step, strike on the way up and cannot be hit for their first few steps (longer for the heavy version). The move holds its airborne pose until the fighter lands, then plays its last segment as the recovery on landing, which is when a missed one is punished.
  - **Dives** leave the ground the same way, rising and drifting forward, and on their dive step turn into a steep kick down and forward at the dive's own speed, striking all the way to the floor. They too hold their airborne pose until they land and recover on landing. Nothing about them is invulnerable, and like any attack from the air the kick must be blocked standing.
  - **Wall leaps** leave the ground travelling *backwards*, away from the opponent, and hold the step before their spring until they reach the edge behind them: the arena wall, or the most one screen lets the fighters part. There they spring off it forwards, and the move jumps to its spring step. Short of the edge, the fighter simply lands into the recovery, wide open. Both are aerial specials in the attack code (`isAerial` in `attacks.ts`), sharing the launch and landing of rising specials; neither adds anything to the fight state, since the move's step and the fighter's speed already say where they are.
  - **Kestrel:** *Talon Dive* (→ + P) springs up and forward from a 4-step crouch and turns into a steep dive kick on step 16, 90 damage (12 chip) as an overhead.   - **Dash specials** carry the fighter along the floor at their own speed from one step of the move to another (the heavy version faster, so further), striking through the move's segments if it has any. A dash can be **projectile-proof**, so projectiles fly straight through the fighter while they travel, and can **pass through** the opponent: neither pushes the other, so the fighter can come out on the far side. Once the dash is over, bodies push apart as usual. A dash can also **stop on contact**: its travel ends the moment it hits or is blocked, like Grom's charge meeting a wall. Knox's dashing punches (step 18) will reuse it.

On a grab step, an opponent in reach (the light version reaches further) who could be thrown is caught, whatever they are pressing: blocking does not help, and neither does the throw break. The throw then plays its own **hold** poses. Part-way through, the opponent is lifted overhead (off the floor, so the bodies do not push, and drawn lying flat, held like a log), and at the end they are tossed forward into a knockdown. A miss plays out the rest of the move, which is a long whiff. An opponent who is reeling, blocking or in the air cannot be caught, so a blocked jab must be waited out before the throw will catch.

- **Rounds** (`src/systems/sim/rounds.ts`, timings in `ROUND` in `config.ts`): the rounds are part of the fight state, so both players online agree on the clock, every KO and every winner.
  - A round goes **intro** ("ROUND 1", or "FINAL ROUND" when both are one win from the match) → **fight** ("FIGHT!", and the clock runs; it stops during hitstop) → **KO** or **TIME** → **result** (the winner's name and win pose, or "DRAW") → the next round, set up afresh, or the end of the match. Controls only count during the fight itself, so nobody can hit a fighter who is already down and out.
  - A KO wins the round, and two at once are a draw ("DOUBLE K.O."). When time runs out, more health wins and equal health is a draw. A drawn round counts for nobody.
  - **Best of three:** the first to two round wins takes the match. After the third round the one with more wins takes it, and equal wins are a **draw game**.
  - The round length and the wins needed are **match rules**, carried in the `MatchSetup` (99 seconds and 2 wins unless changed; the options screen in step 27 will set them). The fight turns the seconds into steps.
  - Once the match is decided, the fight lingers for a moment and then shows the results, which also know a draw game.
- **The HUD** (`src/scenes/hud/FightHud.ts`) is drawn from the state each frame: health bars that lose health from their inner end, next to the clock, with an orange strip that catches up to show recent damage; the round clock between them; names and round-win markers under the bars; the combo count on the side of the one landing it; and the big announcements in the middle.
- **The boxes were checked with an overlay while the fight was built:** push boxes (yellow), hurtboxes (blue), hitboxes (red) and throw reach (green outline), with each move's phase and step, such as `ACTIVE 9/29`, or what held a fighter up (`HITSTUN 11`, `BLOCKSTUN 7`, `DOWN LYING`, `THROW 5`) over their head. A frame advance went with it — one key froze the fight, another ran exactly one step — which is how a move was read frame by frame: Brand's standing heavy kick has no hitbox on steps 1 to 8, one on his foot on steps 9 to 13, and none from 14 on while his leg is still out. **The whole overlay is gone from the finished game**, along with the input panel and the replay key, so nothing debug-ish is left in front of anyone playing it: the fight screen now has the HUD on it and nothing else.
- **Art:** fighters are posed with the shared humanoid rig (`@shared/pixel-art/humanoidRig`), each with their own body, head and palette. Arenas and effects are text pixel maps. There are no image files.

An arm can be drawn with **no hand** (`missingHand`), its forearm ending at the wrist, which is how Mahmood is drawn. A body can wear a **hood down** (`hood`), a rounded bunch of cloth set behind the neck and drawn before the body, like the cape, so the torso and head cover its front and what shows is the lump of it past the fighter's back — a head map cannot do that job, because everything it draws lands inside the body. Sleeves, trousers, fists and feet can each have their own colour instead of the cloth and skin: that is how Brand has bare arms, hand wraps, jeans and boots, and how ten fighters can look like ten different people. A body can also ask for a one-pixel **outline**, which keeps the figures readable against busy arenas.

- **Character select** (`src/scenes/CharacterSelectScene.ts`, drawn by `src/scenes/select/`): a 4 × 4 grid of framed boxes — the fifteen fighters, then the `?` box — with a **cursor per player** and each player's choice previewed at full size on their own side of it, idling and facing the other.
  - **Both players choose at once** rather than taking turns. Each drives their own cursor with the half of the keyboard and the gamepad they will fight with (`SELECT_CONTROLS` in `config.ts`, built from the same key layouts as `PLAYER_CONTROLS`), so there is no second set of controls to learn: the directions move, light punch confirms and light kick goes back. Enter, Space and Esc are player 1's alone — bound to both, one press would have chosen for both players at once. Left and right wrap round within a row and up and down within a column, so the cursor never sticks at an edge. In versus each player hears only their own gamepad; with one player, any pad drives them.
  - **In VS CPU** one cursor makes both picks in turn: the player's own fighter, then the computer's, with the heading changing to SELECT OPPONENT and the cursor to player 2's colour so it is plain which is being chosen. Back steps from the opponent to their own fighter before it leaves the screen. **Arcade** chooses only the player's, since the ladder draws its opponents, and so does **online**, since the other comes from the other browser: that side of the screen is left empty until the match connects. A player who has settled gets a filled cursor and READY under their preview.
  - **How the screen is worked is a rule, not drawing** (`select/selectFlow.ts`): which sides a mode chooses, which one a lone cursor is on, when everyone is ready, and what back does. It holds no Phaser, so a whole sequence of presses can be run through it headlessly — VS CPU settling twice, back stepping from the opponent to your own fighter, player 2's back in versus leaving player 1 settled — which a test inside the scene could not reach.
  - **Portraits** (`src/content/sprites/portraits.ts`) are each fighter from the belt up — head, shoulders and raised fists — cut out of their own idle frame at 30 × 28, so nothing is drawn twice and a new fighter gets one for nothing. At this size the stance says as much about a fighter as the face does, which a head alone in a box did not. Fighters stand as much as five pixels off-centre in their frame, since they lean into the stance and the far arm is drawn behind them, so the cut is **centred on whatever of the fighter falls in those rows** rather than on the frame: that is what puts every portrait in the middle of its box without a hand-written offset per fighter. The widest of them, Grom, is exactly 30 across, which set the size. A sheet carries one palette and each fighter has their own, so every portrait is a sheet of its own; the `?` box is one more, a question mark outlined the same way so it sits on the grid as one of them.
  - **Alternate colours** (`src/content/sprites/altPalette.ts`): when both players pick the same fighter, player 2 wears that fighter's second colours and player 1 keeps the real ones. They are worked out from the fighter's own palette rather than drawn by hand — every colour turned the same distance round the colour wheel, keeping how light and how strong it is, so the shade colours stay darker than what they shade and the figure reads with the same folds in another colour. Three sorts of colour are left alone: the near-black of an outline or an eye and the near-white of a highlight, which would break the drawing rather than recolour it; and **skin**, since an alternate outfit should not make a fighter a different person. Every fighter's head sprite paints the face with `s` and its shade with `S`, which is also what the body gives bare arms and Knox's bare chest, so leaving those two alone covers all of it. A colour too grey to have a hue worth turning is given a little of the new one instead, which is what stops a fighter in white or grey coming back looking the same.
  - A fighter's sheet is every pose they have at 64 × 64, so a second copy of all sixteen would be a great many textures drawn at boot for something most matches never show. The alternate sheets are built **when they are first needed** instead (`ensureAltFighterSprites`), as a cursor lands on the fighter the other player has already taken; registering a sheet twice does nothing, so it is safe to call on any frame. Boot draws 125 sheets rather than 141.
- **CPU** (`src/systems/cpu/`): the CPU produces inputs just like a player. It reacts after a delay and makes mistakes on purpose, both set per difficulty. Each fighter has their own tendencies: Grom walks in for throws, Nova keeps her distance, and Rook waits for you to jump.
  - **Controllers** (`src/systems/input/controller.ts`): whatever drives a fighter — the keyboard and gamepad (`LocalInput`), the CPU, and later the other player online — is asked for one input per step, so the fight cannot tell them apart and the CPU cannot cheat. Its inputs went into the replay check like a player's while that check existed, so CPU fights were covered by it too. In arcade mode player 2 is the CPU.
  - **How it plays** (`cpuPlayer.ts`): it sees its opponent as they were a few steps ago — that delay is its reaction time. On every step it defends first: it may block an attack or projectile it sees coming (once per attack), crouching for lows and standing for overheads if it reads them right, break a throw, or meet a jump-in with an anti-air. Then it carries on with its plan, a queue of inputs played one per step. When free, it thinks up a new plan every few steps: walk in or back off to its preferred distance, a jab chain, a sweep or a throw up close, a poke at mid range, a projectile or a jump-in from afar, and a kick on the way down. Sometimes it does something rash instead. After a normal hits, it may cancel it into a special. A fighter with dash specials uses them too: a striking dash as a mid-range attack, a non-striking one to close in, and a projectile-proof one to go straight through a fireball instead of blocking it.
  - **Special moves come from the fighter's data:** the CPU types in its fighter's motions input by input (`inputPlans.ts`), starting from neutral so the last direction reads as a fresh tap, so every fighter's specials work for it without new code. Up close, a fighter with a command throw goes for it, using the heavy version only when the opponent is well within its shorter reach.
  - **Levels** (`CPU_LEVELS` in `config.ts`): easy, normal and hard differ in reaction time, how often it thinks, and its chances of blocking, reading the guard, anti-airing, breaking throws, making a mistake and comboing. There is a fourth, **boss**, which only Magnus Vane plays at: the fight picks it whenever the CPU's fighter is the boss, whatever the match asked for, since he is the end of arcade mode and not a difficulty setting. He sees a step sooner than the hard CPU and slips a little less often, and that is all — his inputs go through the simulation like anyone else's, so he has no more health, no more damage and no way to cheat. `ChosenCpuLevel` is the three a player may pick, so the options screen (step 27) cannot offer his. **Styles** (`cpuStyle.ts`, one per fighter, such as `brand/brandCpu.ts`): preferred distance, aggression, how often it jumps, throws projectiles and goes for throws. The CPU's randomness is its own and seeded, and lives outside the fight simulation.

- **Online play** (`src/systems/net/`, PeerJS 1.5.5):
  - **A room and a link.** The host's browser makes up a six-character **room code** (`roomCode.ts`) from an alphabet with no look-alike characters, and registers it with the signalling server as `arena-fighters-<code>`, so it cannot collide with another game's rooms. The code is shown on the lobby screen and Enter copies the whole link (`?room=<code>`) to the clipboard. Opening that link starts the game on the fighter select in online mode, and the lobby then knocks at the room. The code is taken out of the address bar once the match starts, so choosing ONLINE again afterwards opens a room of this player's own instead of knocking at one that has gone. The signalling server only introduces the two browsers; everything after that goes straight between them over WebRTC, and nothing is stored anywhere. It is the free public PeerJS server unless `VITE_PEER_HOST` in `.env.local` points to your own (see `.env.example`).
  - **The handshake** (`handshake.ts`): the guest says hello with the fighter it chose, the host measures the round trip three times, picks the input delay from the quickest of them, and names the match — both fighters, the arena its own player picked on the way to the lobby, and the rules. Only the host decides, so there is nothing for the two to disagree about. The host is player 1 and the guest player 2; either way the one player at the keyboard uses the player 1 controls.
  - **Delay-based lockstep** (`lockstep.ts`, `netPlay.ts`): each browser settles its input for the step `delay` steps ahead and sends it at once, and a step is fought only when both inputs for it are in hand. The delay is two to eight steps, from the measured round trip. The opening steps are nobody's, since their inputs were due before the fight began: both sides fill them in the same way rather than sending them, and they are spent on the "ROUND 1" announcement, where the controls do nothing anyway. Neither side can wedge the other, because a side sends the input for a step before fighting the one `delay` steps earlier, so a side that is waiting is always the one further ahead.
  - **Every message is untrusted** (rule 14). `netMessages.ts` checks each one against a strict shape — a known type, whole numbers inside their own limits, an input bitmask of 0–255, and fighters and arenas that are really in the game — and builds the message it hands back rather than passing on the thing that arrived, so nothing can ride along with it. The frame is judged separately by the inbox, which is the only thing that knows which step is being fought: an input is kept from that step up to a window ahead, a checksum from a window behind it, and everything else is dropped. Messages are never put into the page as HTML.
  - **Messages are kept, not dropped, when no screen is listening** (`netInbox.ts`): the other browser sends whenever it likes, and one side can leave the VS screen two seconds before the other. The inbox belongs to the session rather than to a scene, and keeps what arrives by match as well as by frame, so an input that arrives before its fight has opened is waiting when it does.
  - **Desync check:** every second both sides send a checksum of the whole fight state (`checksum.ts`, the same one the replay check uses) and compare the one they are sent. A mismatch ends the match with a message rather than letting the two games drift apart on two screens.
  - **Disconnects and quiet lines:** a step whose opponent's input has not arrived holds the fight where it is, says WAITING FOR OPPONENT after a second, and gives the match up after ten, which is longer than the VS screen one side can skip. A tab that closes, a connection that fails and a room that does not exist each end with a line of their own.
  - **The session outlives the screens** (`netSession.ts`): the two fighters meet on the VS screen, fight, see the results and may fight again, so the connection cannot belong to any one scene. There is one session for the page, opened when the two browsers agree on a match and closed however the player leaves.
  - **Rematches are agreed, not demanded:** the results screen offers REMATCH and QUIT, and the next fight starts only once both sides have asked for it. Changing fighters is not offered, since that would mean agreeing a whole new match, which is what the lobby is for. Each fight of a session is numbered, and that number is part of every input, so the side that agrees first cannot have its first inputs mistaken for the fight the other side is still finishing.
  - The build CSP (`tooling/csp.ts`) is extended by the signalling server's address, and only on this game's page: the other games and the console menu stay shut in to their own origin.
  - **Tested** three ways. The messages, the inbox's window and the lockstep were run outside a browser: every malformed message is dropped, and two sides fighting 400 steps at every delay from 2 to 8, with lag and with one side on a slower machine, always fight exactly the same steps and never wedge. The real fight simulation was then run on both sides of a simulated line, which stays in step over 4000 steps, notices a nudged copy within a couple of seconds, and gives a silent opponent up. Finally two headless browsers played a real match through the public signalling server: the host opened a room, the guest joined by link, both reached the same frame of the same fight, and closing the guest's tab left the host waiting and then back at the title.
- **Sound and music** (`src/content/sounds.ts`, `src/content/music/`, `src/systems/fightSounds.ts`): everything is synthesised by `@shared/audio`, so the game ships no audio files, exactly as it ships no image files. **M** mutes everything, on every screen.
  - **The fight is heard, not played.** The simulation is pure — a state and two inputs in, the next state out — so it cannot make a noise without stopping being pure. Instead `FightSounds` is handed every state the fight passes through and compares it with the one before: health lost is a blow landing, a guard raised is a block, a status turning to `knockdown` is a fighter going over, and so on. Three things fall out of that. A whole fight can be re-run in silence — the replay check did exactly that — since re-running never goes through the observer. The simulation stays as testable as it was. And an **online match sounds the same in both browsers without a single sound being sent**, because both sides are watching the same states go by.
  - **The ear can tell what happened without looking.** A swing that finds nothing is air alone — filtered noise, high and dry for a fist, lower and wider for a leg; a blow that lands adds a pitched thump that falls away, and a heavy one (`AUDIO.heavyHitDamage` or more) puts the floor in it; a block is that thump with the crack taken out. Specials are pitched where normals are not, so they stand out: a projectile leaves the hand and runs away from the ear, a rising special goes up, a teleport arrives before its fighter does. A knockdown is two sounds, the fall and then the floor.
  - **The announcer has horns instead of a voice.** There are no voice samples to record and none to fake, so the round being called, FIGHT, the knockout, time running out, a round taken and the match taken are all short jingles, each with its own notes. A heal deliberately does *not* use the notes of a round won, so being healed is never mistaken for having won something.
  - **A loop for each of the sixteen arenas** (`content/music/arenaMusic.ts`), each thirty-two steps of a tune over a bass, taking its key and its speed from the place: the docks brood in D minor, the plaza dances in A, the mine is slow and low, the power station runs in broken chords, the monastery leaves the room almost empty, the foundry is driven by a bass that never lets up, and the tower roof is slower and heavier than anything under it, with a third voice holding the chord. The screens between fights have their own loops (`screenMusic.ts`), quieter than the arenas because somebody is reading over them — except the one for choosing, which is a fanfare in D minor over a marching bass, a bar to each chord (the home chord, the one below it, the one below that, and a dominant carrying the leading note, which will not sit still and pulls the loop back round). Choosing a mode, a fighter and an arena is the build-up to the tournament rather than a wait before it, so it is the one screen loop written to announce something. Its tune and the harmony under it are held for two steps and four, the way a horn is, which is what keeps a fanfare from sounding like a list of notes.
  - **Loops have no seam.** The shared player hands notes to the Web Audio clock a little ahead of time rather than restarting a sound at the end of each loop, so the loop boundary is just another step. Asking for the track that is already playing changes nothing, which is what lets the select music carry across the mode, fighter and arena screens without restarting. The arena's loop stops as the match is decided, so the winner's jingle has the room to itself.
  - **Tested** two ways. Outside a browser, with the Web Audio API replaced by something that writes down what it is asked to play: every one of the transitions above was checked to make a sound, and the *right* sound; no two sounds in the game turned out to be the same sound; a whole CPU-against-CPU match was fought and every everyday action — both punches, both kicks, hits, blocks, jumps, landings, throws, knockdowns and the calls of the round — was heard in it; and all twenty-two loops were checked for notes in range, notes inside the loop, and something under the tune. Then in a real browser: the audio stays held until the first key press and wakes on it, the music keeps feeding the clock while nothing else is happening, a fight adds tones and bursts of noise on top, and M silences the lot.
- **Title, options, controls and pause** (`src/scenes/TitleScene.ts`, `OptionsScene.ts`, `ControlsScene.ts`, `PauseScene.ts`, settings in `src/systems/settings.ts`): the title opens a menu rather than a match — START, OPTIONS and CONTROLS — and the same controls table is shown again from the pause menu, so there is one table rather than two that can drift apart.
  - **A setting is a menu item with a value.** The shared menu (`@shared/phaser/menu`) already drew a list with a cursor; an item may now also carry a value and answer to left and right, which is all an options screen is. It is read from the keyboard and from a gamepad at once, as every menu in the console is, and confirm steps a setting forward as well, so a screen of settings can be worked with a single button. Nothing else about the menu changed, so the same code still draws the title, the modes, the results and the pause menu.
  - **What the options hold is what a match is made of.** Round time and the number of rounds become the `MatchRules` a new match is started with, and the difficulty is the level VS CPU hands the CPU — the arcade ladder sets its own, rung by rung, as it climbs. VS CPU still asks the question before a match, since that is where it is most wanted, but it opens on the saved level and a different answer becomes the new setting: one difficulty shown in two places, rather than two that can disagree.
  - **What comes back from storage is not believed.** The settings live in `localStorage`, which is as much outside the game as anything that arrives over the network: it can hold text that is not JSON, a number, a list, a level the game does not offer or a round time of a hundred thousand seconds. Every field is checked against the choices the screen offers and anything else falls back to the default, so a corrupt entry costs the player their settings and nothing more. Storage can also refuse outright (a private window, blocked site data), and then the game plays on with the defaults and simply forgets the settings when it closes.
  - **The pause menu is laid over the fight, not instead of it.** The fight scene is paused and the menu scene launched on top, so the arena is still there behind it, dimmed, and nothing about the fight has to be saved and rebuilt. The arena's music stops while the menu is open and starts again on the way out. Esc or Start opens it; resume, the controls table, and quitting to the title are what it offers.
  - **There is no pause in an online match.** The other browser cannot be stopped, and a fight stopped on one side alone would only fall out of step, so the fight scene does not offer the menu at all when it is being played online. Esc there does nothing.
  - **Tested** in a real browser, driven by the keyboard *and* by a gamepad — a fake pad on `navigator.getGamepads`, which the game polls every frame exactly as it would a real one. Every setting was changed with the pad alone and found in storage afterwards; the settings survived a reload and were still on screen; a round time of 30 seconds and a best of five reached the clock and the round markers of the fight that followed; the pause menu opened with the pad's Start button and with Esc, and froze the fight, which two screenshots taken a second and a half apart proved by being the same picture down to the byte, while two taken after resuming were not. Sound off was checked where it happens — the game's own output node, turned to zero and back. Away from the browser, the settings were fed nonsense of every shape storage can hold, and a storage that refuses to answer at all. In an online match between two browsers, Esc was pressed mid-fight and both sides fought on.
- **Dev shortcut:** in `npm run dev`, add `?scene=Fight` (or another scene name) to jump straight to a scene, and `&p1=brand&p2=grom` to pick the matchup, which is fought in player 2's home arena unless `&stage=docks` picks another. `&roundSeconds=5&roundsToWin=1` shortens a match for testing, `&cpu=hard` sets the CPU level. With `&mode=arcade` the setup carries a real ladder, so `&rung=7` starts on a given rung: `?scene=Story&mode=arcade&p1=tala&rung=7` shows Tala's ending and `?scene=Fight&mode=arcade&p1=tala&rung=7` is the boss fight. `&seed=` picks which shuffle, and `&p1cpu=normal` makes player 1 a CPU too, to watch two CPUs fight (`?scene=Fight&mode=arcade&p1cpu=normal`).
